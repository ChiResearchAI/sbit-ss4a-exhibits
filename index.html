Method exhibit · Component A

Counting before mapping

What the federal crash record can and cannot support for a High-Injury Network on the Shoalwater Bay Indian Reservation.

ChiResearch Technologies, LLC · Prepared for the Shoalwater Bay Indian Tribe, RFP P-2027-2 · October 2026

What this document is. Original applied analysis built entirely from NHTSA's current public Fatality Analysis Reporting System release, prepared as an example of relevant work product under the Proposal Requirements of RFP P-2027-2. It is analysis produced for this bid, not work delivered under a client contract. Every figure here is computed from the cited release by the method stated in Section 7 and reproduces from the scripts that produced it.

Component A asks for a geospatial identification of higher-risk locations — "a high-injury network or equivalent." The standard way to build one is to rank road segments by observed crash frequency or crash density and take the worst performers. That method needs enough crashes to rank.

Before proposing a method, we counted. This note reports what the count turned out to be, what it rules out, and what it requires instead.

1. The count, at the scale of the reservation

The Shoalwater Bay Indian Reservation is approximately one square mile on the north shore of Willapa Bay, bisected east–west by SR 105. We measured straight-line distance from the Shoalwater Bay Tribal Center to every fatal crash in Washington State with usable coordinates, 2019 through 2023, and counted by radius.

The single fatal crash within five miles occurred on SR 105 in 2022, 2.3 miles from the Tribal Center, on a roadway owned by the State Highway Agency. Across all of Pacific County the five-year total is 18 fatal crashes and 20 deaths, distributed 6 / 1 / 1 / 5 / 5 across the five years.

Is this a Pacific County peculiarity?

No, and we checked rather than assuming. Computing the same count for all 39 Washington counties on the same data: the median county recorded 32 fatal crashes over the five years, the range runs from 1 to 635, and 11 of 39 counties recorded 20 or fewer. Pacific County, at 18, ranks tenth from the bottom. The small-count problem is a structural property of rural safety planning in Washington, not a quirk of this jurisdiction — which is why the method below is a method and not a workaround.

2. What the small count does to a systemic analysis

Component A also asks for "analysis of systemic and specific safety needs … high-risk road features." The systemic approach asks a different question from the frequency approach: not where have crashes happened, but which road features are over-represented among the crashes that have. That question tolerates a smaller sample than a frequency ranking does. It does not tolerate an arbitrarily small one.

We tested five candidate risk factors in the 25-mile study area against the same measure computed the same way on all 3,084 geocoded Washington fatal crashes. The statewide figure is the baseline; the study-area figure is the test.

Wilson score interval on the study-area proportion; Fisher's exact test, two-sided, against the statewide count. Factors are FARS-coded fields: relation to roadway, manner of collision, intersection type, and light condition.

The number that sets the scope of work. To detect a difference of the size observed on off-roadway departure — 44.4% against a 32.7% baseline — at 80% power and a 5% significance level requires approximately 131 crashes in the study area. The fatal-only record supplies 36. The analysis is short by a factor of about 3.6.

3. Where the missing records are

FARS is a census of fatal crashes and nothing else. It contains serious-injury records only for people injured in a crash where someone died — in Washington's 2023 file, 184 persons coded "Suspected Serious Injury (A)" appear, every one of them inside a fatal crash. Crashes in which someone was seriously injured and no one died are not in the dataset at any radius or any time span.

That is the gap, and it is also the remedy. SS4A defines its safety baseline on fatalities and serious injuries. The serious-injury half of that baseline lives in the Washington State crash database held by WSDOT and the Washington Traffic Safety Commission, not in the federal release. Obtaining it is the first task of the Safety Analysis, not an optional enrichment — and on any plausible ratio of serious-injury to fatal crashes it moves the study-area sample from 36 into the range where the systemic test above has the power to return an answer rather than an interval.

4. Two findings about the data itself

The reservation is not flagged in the federal record

FARS carries a special-jurisdiction field that identifies crashes on Indian reservation land. Across Washington, 172 fatal crashes in 2019–2023 are coded that way, spread over 14 counties — 79 in Yakima County, 46 in Pierce. In Pacific County the count is zero, across all 18 fatal crashes, on a reservation that SR 105 runs directly through.

We do not assert why. What follows operationally is firm regardless: an analyst who scopes this work by filtering on reservation jurisdiction retrieves an empty table and may conclude there is no safety problem to analyze. The study area has to be defined geographically — by corridor and by radius — and jurisdiction treated as an attribute to be verified against the Tribe's own records, never as the filter that builds the dataset.

Four road owners inside the study area

The RFP requires that "the analysis should include all roadways within the jurisdiction, without regard for ownership." On this data that is not boilerplate. The 36 crashes in the study area are distributed across four distinct road-owner categories in the federal coding alone:

5. The Tribe reached this conclusion in 2017

After completing the analysis above we read the Tribe's own Shoalwater Bay Indian Tribe Safety Plan (May 2017), published on the Tribe's website. It describes the same obstacle, found the same way, nine years earlier:

"Crash Data was requested from Washington State Department of Transportation (WSDOT) — Transportation Data and GIS Office (TDGO). Due to limited crash data from WSDOT, the Shoalwater Bay Tribal Police Department was requested to provide any crash data they had. In discussions with the Tribe, it was determined that, due to a lack of information, a Road Safety Audit (RSA) needed to be completed on various roads and intersections within the Shoalwater Bay Reservation."

"While much of the crash data we have researched is not specific to the geographic area of the Shoalwater Bay Reservation, and not accurate to the unique characteristics of the SBIT Tribal Transportation System and community…"

That is the finding of Sections 1 through 3 of this note, in the Tribe's own words, reached from the State dataset rather than the federal one. We did not know it when we ran the analysis. It is the strongest available evidence that the small-count problem is a durable property of this jurisdiction and not an artifact of the window or the source we happened to choose — and it is why the Tribe's first emphasis area in that plan is Data Management, ahead of any engineering countermeasure.

Two further points of contact between that plan and this analysis are worth stating, because they run in opposite directions.

Where the 2017 plan corroborates the systemic finding

The one risk factor elevated above the statewide baseline in Section 2 is off-roadway departure. The Tribe's fourth emphasis area is "Reduction of Roadside Hazards", and the plan documents the specific condition behind it: on the southwest side of Tokeland Road (Route 9513), a ditch roughly two feet deep at a 0.5:1 slope running some 780 feet near the Tribal Center complex, with a further stretch of about 1,000 feet by the police station. A field audit and a federal crash dataset arrived at the same place independently. Neither establishes the finding alone; together they are a reason to carry it into the systemic screening as a prior rather than discover it again.

Where the 2017 plan shows what the federal record cannot see

The same plan records 11 crashes in the Casino parking lot between October 2011 and December 2015, 7 of them involving pedestrians, with the remainder hit-and-run, alongside 419 traffic stops reported across the Reservation. None of those 11 crashes appears anywhere in the analysis above, at any radius or any window — because nobody died in them, and FARS is a census of fatal crashes only.

That is the Section 3 gap made concrete on the Tribe's own ground. A pedestrian-crash cluster in a single parking lot, documented by the Tribe, invisible to the federal record. It is also the clearest argument for the data architecture this Safety Analysis proposes: the serious-injury and non-fatal record exists, it is held by WSDOT and by the Shoalwater Bay Tribal Police Department, and the 2017 plan already names the Police Department as the source that had to be approached when the State data fell short.

A note on one figure: the 2017 plan states the pedestrian share of those 11 crashes twice, once as 70% and once as 64%. Seven of eleven is 63.6%. We cite the counts rather than either percentage, and we flag it because a Safety Action Plan that carries a contradiction forward into a grant application inherits it. Reconciling figures of this kind across the Tribe's prior plans is part of Task 1 as we have scoped it.

7. Method, and its limits

Every figure above is computed from the FARS annual national CSV releases for 2019 through 2023, filtered to Washington State (FIPS 53), retrieved 21 October 2026. Distances are straight-line great-circle distances from an approximate Tribal Center coordinate of 46.708 N, 124.044 W, computed on the crash coordinates published in the release. County counts use the FARS county coding. Proportions are tested with Fisher's exact test, two-sided; intervals are Wilson score intervals. The sample-size requirement in Section 2 is a two-proportion calculation at 80% power and α = 0.05 against a comparison group large enough to treat as a known rate.

The limits, stated because they bear on how much weight each figure carries:

  • Straight-line distance is not network distance. A crash 2.3 miles from the Tribal Center as the crow flies may be considerably further by road. The radius bands are a counting device for establishing sample size, not a corridor definition. The corridor study area in the proposal is defined by route and milepost, which is what the Safety Analysis would actually use.
  • Eight of 3,092 Washington crashes lack usable coordinates and are excluded from the distance analysis but retained in the county and statewide counts. The exclusion is reported rather than absorbed.
  • FARS is fatal-only. Section 3 is the whole of what can be said about serious injuries from this source, and it is a statement about the dataset rather than about the corridor.
  • The approximate centroid is ours, not the Tribe's. It is derived from the published Tribal Center address. A reservation boundary polygon from the Tribe's own GIS would shift every band slightly; it would not change the finding that the counts are 0, 1 and 3 at two, five and ten miles.
  • Five years is a choice. We ran 2019–2023 because those are the complete annual releases available. A ten-year window would roughly double the sample and dilute recency; the proposal commits to reporting the window's effect on the result rather than selecting the window that produces the preferred answer.
